# What leftists and progressives miss about AI
## Introduction
I often get into discussions (read: arguments) with leftists of all stripes over AI.  Most Americans, if not most people around the western world, have only heard about AI from western media sources or straight from the various AI technofascists' mouths.  And they are absolutely in the right when it comes to a critique of those individuals and institutions, yet the field and industry of AI is much larger than just the US.  

Namely, China and the Chinese AI model offer such a radically different vision for AI that I wish more people could understand in which there's a way of creating and deploying this technology with both the people and the planet at the forefront. 

In discussing the tech, I am intentionally omitting any of the positive AI fluff pieces to keep this focused on comparing the social models vs trying to defend the technology in its own right.  Moreover, I believe that the Chinese model also fundamentally undermines the US AI companies (meaning their success also means an end to the US AI hegemony), but that is out of scope for this document.   

Therefore, I'm writing this piece first and foremost to illuminate the contradictions in our western model and the material alternatives already improving lives here and abroad with the Chinese model.  

## The Communist AI Approach

Technically the Chinese model is Socialism with Chinese characteristics, but this sounds catchier.

The Chinese AI model is so different in its outcomes because the economic mode of production (socialism vs capitalism) changes the incentives and distribution of profits.  But more fundamentally, the relationship between the state and capital is a dictatorship of the proletariat. This means the working class controls the Chinese government rather than the wealthy ruling class that we see in liberal democracies.

As such, the goals of the technology are centered upon achieving harmony with humanity and the planet rather than making profits under capitalism.  I will look at a number of categories including environmental, social, etc. which offer alternative visions for how this technology could be used under a different socioeconomic regime.

Tl;dr: AI and robotics aren't destroying jobs, the planet, or recreating a two tier society — it's our capitalist economic model that is.   Capitalism has found a set of technologies which it can use to replace its dependence on human labor whereas socialism has discovered a technology to move humanity collectively forward beyond ["bullshit jobs"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullshit_Jobs) [[128](#source-128)] and meaningless labor.   

That said, as a scientist and a dialectical materialist, I prefer "objective" facts and analysis to conjecture, so I will list both positive and negative aspects of the Chinese model throughout this piece, especially with some of the social ramifications.

Transparently, much of the rest of this piece is co-written with my local AI setup on a renewable energy provider (i.e. zero water used, and no emissions).  All high level points are my own, but the majority of the research and some of the writeup are generated.  

I'll update this piece as needed as I verify the various sources.

### A Brief Introduction to the Chinese Governance Model

Most people are never taught about the governance structures of non-liberal democratic countries, so I'll briefly introduce the primary economic difference.  Namely, one party central planning by the government vs the corporate-government planning that we see in most western countries.  These take the shape of five year plans which use the public and private sectors to implement the various priorities of the party + working class. 

Without any corporate or market influences, the government can directly shape the development of the country for the collective benefit of the people.  Through this system, China has been able to lift [900 million people out of poverty more rapidly than any other nation in history](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/nov/23/china-us-poverty-income-inequality) [[1](#source-1)] in deep contrast to the US, which builds its economy upon poverty.  Similarly, the rapid and somewhat mindboggling advancements that China continues to achieve in AI, robotics, biotech, EVs, renewable energy, and more are all a by-product of this five year central planning system over the last eight decades.

## On the Environment + Data Centers
In the west, we often hear of AI data center expansion largely as a neocolonial project by big tech companies which are paying near-unlimited amounts of money to build data centers in locations with the most exploitable resources whether energy, water, or human talent.

Yet if you pay attention to any of these data center exposes, they're never of any major Chinese firm.   There's a variety of state mandated reasons for this, but many Chinese firms would never want to build in America even without these restrictions.  The aging, outdated energy infrastructure of the US is so brittle that it's nearly impossible to optimize the energy grid to support and run data centers cost effectively whether by renewable energy or smart energy transportation.   This is why you see so many [hyperscaler data centers building their own fossil fuel infrastructure](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/25/us-gas-power-china-ai-datacenter) [[9](#source-9)] rather than solely using grid energy.

Conversely, China is one of the only nations in the world to actually modernize its energy grid, allowing energy transportation across thousands of miles from energy rich areas to energy hungry areas, enabling a nationally coordinated energy model to reduce the disparate environmental impacts.

As detailed below, the majority of emissions (85%) and water use (55%) that you hear about in the headlines actually come from the energy grid rather than the data center itself (i.e.  we use massive amounts of coal and natural gas in America since we don't have nearly as much renewable solar and wind domestically).  Moreover, given China has added more renewable energy to the grid just last year than the next 5 years of expected US data center energy use combined. This incredibly rapid pace of solar energy and energy distribution enables a much more sustainable deployment of AI infrastructure without forcing future generations to pay the carbon debts of a collapsing environment.

### Data center vs. power plant: energy and water consumption
- China's data centers consumed ~200 TWh (terawatt-hours — 1 TWh is roughly the annual electricity use of 100,000 US homes) in 2020 (2.7% of national electricity); projected to reach 400 TWh by 2030 (3.7% of national power) ([Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-how-china-is-managing-the-rising-energy-demand-from-data-centres) [[13](#source-13)]; [CAICT](https://www.caict.ac.cn/kxyj/qwfb/ztbg/202505/P020250509511369626787.pdf) [[28](#source-28)])
- China accounted for 25% of global data-center electricity consumption in 2024 (IEA), second only to the US ([Columbia CKI](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/CKI/data%20centers%20CKI/CKI%20AI%20x%20Data%20Centers-260123.pdf) [[25](#source-25)]; [Thinking China](https://www.thinkchina.sg/technology/chinese-tech-giants-struggle-power-ai-data-centre-boom) [[31](#source-31)])
- Water consumption: estimated ~1.3bn m³ today, could rise to >3bn m³ by 2030 ([CWR](https://cwrrr.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/CWR-2024-China-ICT-running-dry-The-rise-of-AI-climate-risks-amplify-existing-water-risks-faced-by-thirsty-data-centres.pdf) [[15](#source-15)]; [SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/science/article/3259230/chinas-growing-data-centres-and-ai-industry-could-strain-scarce-water-resources-according-new-report) [[39](#source-39)])
- 46% of data center racks are in the "Dry 10" water-scarce provinces ([CWR](https://cwrrr.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/CWR-2024-China-ICT-running-dry-The-rise-of-AI-climate-risks-amplify-existing-water-risks-faced-by-thirsty-data-centres.pdf) [[15](#source-15)])
- PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness — the ratio of total power a data center consumes to the power actually used for computing, where 1.0 means every watt goes to the machines and 2.0 means half is lost to cooling and overhead) target: cut large data centers to 1.25 by 2025 (Germany requires 1.5 by 2027) ([Applied Energy](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/appene/v399y2025ics0306261925012528.html) [[16](#source-16)]; [Gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/statecouncil/ministries/202112/09/content_WS61b13edac6d09c94e48a1f81.html) [[29](#source-29)]; [NDRC/MIIT/NEA/NDA](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202407/content_6962214.htm) [[30](#source-30)])
- **The "data center vs. power plant" split — Microsoft's Nature paper (2025):**
  - Microsoft researchers published the first public cradle-to-grave life cycle assessment (LCA, an accounting of all environmental impacts across a product's full life from raw materials to disposal) of data center cooling, comparing four cooling technologies (air-cooled, cold-plate, one-phase immersion, two-phase immersion) across GHG (greenhouse gas — heat-trapping emissions like CO2 that drive climate change) emissions and energy demand ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08832-3) [[17](#source-17)]; [Microsoft press release](https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/sustainability/microsoft-quantifies-environmental-impacts-of-datacenter-cooling-from-cradle-to-grave-in-new-Nature-study/) [[18](#source-18)]).
  - They also compared blue water (fresh surface and groundwater, as opposed to "grey" water already in use) consumption across the same four cooling technologies.
  - **The critical finding: switching from grid electricity to 100% renewable energy reduces GHG emissions by 85–90%, energy demand by 6–7%, and blue water consumption by 55–85% — regardless of cooling technology.** This dwarfs the 15–21% GHG savings from switching cooling methods.
  - The paper identifies the "use phase" — the electricity consumed to operate servers — as the largest contributor to environmental impacts for all cooling technologies. "Average grid electricity is still heavily dependent on fossil fuels." Thermal power generation (coal, gas) also requires water to convert to steam.
  - **Implication for the article's argument:** The environmental impact of AI data centers is overwhelmingly determined by the energy source, not the data center design. Building "green" or "sustainable" data centers (better cooling, lower PUE) yields modest gains (15–21% GHG). But decarbonizing the grid — the broader renewable energy buildout — is the dominant lever, accounting for 85–90% of potential emissions reduction. This reframes the debate: the question is not "how do we build cleaner data centers?" but "how fast can we decarbonize the power grid?"
  - **Water follows the same logic:** renewable electricity requires far less water for generation than thermal power. Switching to 100% renewable energy reduces blue water consumption by 55–85%, compared to 31–52% from switching cooling technologies. The water savings from grid decarbonization are roughly 2x the savings from data center cooling innovation.
  - Microsoft's own data: FY25 total energy consumption up 168% from 2020, but Scope 1+2 emissions (Scope 1 = emissions from the company's own fuel use; Scope 2 = emissions from the electricity it buys) down 29.9% from 2020 baseline — because they contracted 34 GW of carbon-free electricity across 24 countries (18x increase since 2020). The energy went up; the emissions went down. The grid, not the data center, is the lever ([Microsoft 2025 ESR](https://cdn-dynmedia-1.microsoft.com/is/content/microsoftcorp/microsoft/msc/documents/presentations/CSR/2025-Microsoft-Environmental-Sustainability-Report.pdf) [[19](#source-19)]; [2026 Fact Sheet](https://cdn-dynmedia-1.microsoft.com/is/content/microsoftcorp/microsoft/msc/documents/presentations/CSR/2026-Microsoft-Environmental-Data-Fact-Sheet-PDF.pdf) [[20](#source-20)]).
  - **China's "East Data, West Computing" initiative is essentially a grid-decarbonization strategy for AI:** by moving compute to the western regions where the bulk of new renewable capacity is being built, China is effectively routing AI workloads to cleaner electricity. This is structurally more impactful than any data center design innovation.
- **IEA corroboration:**
  - Data centers consumed 415 TWh globally in 2024 (1.5% of world electricity); projected to double to ~945 TWh by 2030 (3% of global electricity) ([IEA Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary) [[21](#source-21)]; [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/ai-data-centers-environment-climate-footprint-a792f184a9f2833b5388dbae8b41ca95) [[26](#source-26)]).
  - Renewables are the fastest-growing source of data center electricity, meeting nearly 50% of the growth in data center demand to 2030. In China, renewables + nuclear are expected to push coal into decline for data center operations by 2035 ([IEA Energy supply for AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai) [[22](#source-22)]).
  - CO2 emissions from data center electricity peak at ~320 Mt CO2 (megatonnes of CO2 — a million metric tons, roughly the annual output of 60–80 typical 1,000 MW coal plants) by 2030, then enter a shallow decline — because the grid is decarbonizing faster than data center demand is growing ([IEA Key Questions](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) [[23](#source-23)]).
  - Data centers remain "a relatively small part of the overall power system" — rising from ~1% of global electricity generation today to 3% in 2030, accounting for less than 1% of total global CO2 emissions.
  - **The broader renewable energy buildout is the dominant emissions factor.** Global power sector emissions are plateauing as renewables overtake coal. Renewables and nuclear are expected to account for ~50% of global electricity by 2030 ([IEA Electricity 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/executive-summary) [[24](#source-24)]). Data center emissions (~300 Mt CO2) are a small fraction of total power sector emissions — and the grid is the thing being decarbonized [[24](#source-24)].

### On the path to becoming the world's first electrostate: China's multipronged approach to carbon neutrality
In contrast to the US approach of building ever more fossil fuel based power plants, China is attempting to pioneer fully renewable, local power grids (via microgrids) in addition to the larger buildout of the renewable based grid.   
- "East Data, West Computing" initiative (2022): channel computing from energy-hungry east to wind/solar-rich west, where the bulk of new renewable capacity is being built ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2025-05-16/in-depth-chinese-tech-giants-struggle-to-power-ai-data-center-boom-102320408.html) [[27](#source-27)]; [SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3338522/china-vows-ramp-west-east-power-output-ai-hi-tech-manufacturing-fuel-demand) [[32](#source-32)])

- Tencent's Huailai microgrid (wind + solar + battery) covers only 15% of annual demand; bulk still from state grid ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2025-03-22/ai-boom-puts-pressure-on-chinas-power-grid-as-data-centers-eat-up-electricity-102300894.html) [[14](#source-14)]; [Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2025-05-16/in-depth-chinese-tech-giants-struggle-to-power-ai-data-center-boom-102320408.html) [[27](#source-27)])

### Cross-province energy transport infrastructure
Similarly, since China has undertaken the massive unprofitable, but necessary upgrade to their electrical grid, they can reliably and safely transport energy across the nation.  The US, by contrast, does not operate a single national grid but three largely independent interconnections — the Eastern, the Western, and ERCOT (Texas) — linked by only a handful of small HVDC ties, so bulk power cannot be moved coast-to-coast (e.g., from California to New York) at scale; the 2021 Texas blackout, in which ERCOT could import only ~6% of its demand, shows how limited cross-region transfer really is ([US EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=27152) [[126](#source-126)]).  China, by contrast, has built the world's largest ultra-high-voltage (UHV) network — 42 operational UHV AC/DC projects by end-2024, including the 3,324 km Changji–Guquan (±1,100 kV, 12 GW) and 2,383 km Jiuquan–Xiangtan lines — specifically to ship renewable power from the resource-rich west to the load centers of the east ([Xinhua](https://english.news.cn/20260113/3e3a7d6df5744fefb18b98fa31a4addb/c.html) [[127](#source-127)]).

- Western provinces hold the bulk of new renewable capacity but most computing demand remains in the east ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2025-05-16/in-depth-chinese-tech-giants-struggle-to-power-ai-data-center-boom-102320408.html) [[27](#source-27)])
- Companies split operations: western centers for training energy-intensive AI models, eastern centers for customer-facing applications ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2025-05-16/in-depth-chinese-tech-giants-struggle-to-power-ai-data-center-boom-102320408.html) [[27](#source-27)])
- New policies may allow off-site renewable generation to ease constraints ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3338522/china-vows-ramp-west-east-power-output-ai-hi-tech-manufacturing-fuel-demand) [[32](#source-32)])

### US vs. China: the energy buildout divergence
The two countries have quite divergent energy views, including nuclear ambitions with the US rolling forward with a massive buildout of Uranium based SMRs which produce a great deal of nuclear waste + water consumption, whereas China is pursuing next gen thorium reactors to move safer + cleaner nuclear power.  

While I'm not a nuclear energy proponent in the slightest, it seems foolhardy to pursue legacy nuclear power systems for the sake of quick profits vs long term water scarcity and nuclear waste proliferation.

- **The core asymmetry:** The US is building AI data centers faster than it can build clean power. China is building clean power faster than it can build data centers. The result is two fundamentally different energy trajectories for the same technology ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2025-07-28/us-and-china-diverge-on-strategies-to-power-their-ai-data-centers) [[57](#source-57)]).
- **US data center demand:**
  - [IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary) [[21](#source-21)]: US data center electricity demand set to more than double from 2024 to 2030, reaching 426 TWh (~9% of total US electricity demand). US data centers already account for 45% of global data center electricity consumption ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA3845-1.html) [[46](#source-46)]).
  - [LBNL (2025)](https://seta.lbl.gov/publications/united-states-data-center-energy-2025) [[45](#source-45)]: US data centers could account for 11.8% of total US electricity by 2030 (range: 9.5–15.3%); 649 TWh in the Reference Case (range: 521–843 TWh) [[45](#source-45)].
  - [EPRI (2026)](https://powering-intelligence.epri.com/load-impacts.html) [[44](#source-44)]: US data center peak load growth is substantial through 2030. Under reference policies, "incremental supply is dominated by natural gas."
  - BloombergNEF: Total global data center consumption set to quadruple to 1,600 TWh/year over the next decade ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2025-07-28/us-and-china-diverge-on-strategies-to-power-their-ai-data-centers) [[57](#source-57)]).
  - [Reuters (Feb 2026)](https://www.reuters.com/markets/commodities/us-ai-boom-faces-electric-shock-2026-02-25/) [[58](#source-58)]: Cleanview identified 46 US data centers planning to build their own power plants, primarily gas-fired. Their combined 56 GW represents ~30% of all planned US data center capacity [[58](#source-58)].
- **US energy mix for data centers (IEA):**
  - Natural gas is the largest source of additional supply for US data centers through 2030 ([IEA Energy supply for AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai) [[22](#source-22)]).
  - 2030–2035: SMRs (Small Modular Reactors, i.e. small nuclear power plants) enter the mix (tech companies have plans to finance >20 GW of SMRs). By 2035, low-emissions sources account for >55% of US data center electricity ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai) [[22](#source-22)]; [NucNet](https://www.nucnet.org/news/us-and-china-to-lead-growth-in-nuclear-power-for-data-centre-supply-4-4-2025) [[59](#source-59)]).
  - **The gas turbine bottleneck:** GE Vernova's order book for gas turbines at end of 2025 is 80 GW, with a backlog lasting into 2029. Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries are in the same situation. A project ordering turbines today is unlikely to come online until 2030 or beyond. [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/electricity-supply-bottleneck-us-ai-dominance) [[42](#source-42)]: "The gas generation boom is creating upstream supply chain constraints" [[42](#source-42)].
  - **The near-term result:** [Rhodium Group (2025)](https://rhg.com/research/data-centers-electricity-demand/) [[43](#source-43)]: "Since not much new capacity can be added in the near term, we instead see existing gas and coal plants running at higher capacity factors and meeting 55–85% of increased demand relative to baseline data center demand growth." (Capacity factors are the share of a plant's maximum output that it actually produces over a period — 100% means running flat-out around the clock.) In the high-emissions scenario, "gas dominates additions through 2035 while renewables struggle to compete."
  - **Political dimension:** Trump has championed coal to meet rising data center demand. The US approach is market-driven, fragmented by state permitting, and dependent on corporate PPAs (power purchase agreements — long-term contracts in which a company buys electricity directly from a power generator) — raising risk of grid bottlenecks in major data center clusters.
- **China's renewable buildout (the counterpoint):**
  - 2024: China installed 357 GW (gigawatts — a unit of *power capacity*, i.e. the maximum output a plant can produce at any instant; a 1 GW plant running flat-out for a year would supply ~1 million homes, but in practice it runs at a 30–50% capacity factor, so 1 GW of *installed* capacity serves far fewer homes than its nameplate suggests) of solar and wind in a single year (277 GW solar, 80 GW wind) — a 45% and 18% increase over 2023. [AP](https://apnews.com/article/wind-solar-energy-china-climate-carbon-emissions-b337503abfacfd9b7829fd7bbcd507e9) [[47](#source-47)]: "That's akin to building 357 full-size nuclear plants in one year." China surpassed its 1,200 GW renewable target six years early ([NEA](http://www.nea.gov.cn/20250121/097bfd7c1cd3498897639857d86d5dac/c.html) [[48](#source-48)]; [IEA](https://www.iea.org/data-and-statistics/charts/solar-pv-and-wind-net-additions-in-china-2024-2025) [[55](#source-55)]).
  - 2025: China installed a record 430+ GW of solar and wind (315 GW solar, 119 GW wind), a 22% YoY increase. [IEA](https://www.iea.org/data-and-statistics/charts/solar-pv-and-wind-net-additions-in-china-2024-2025) [[55](#source-55)]: "nearly 500 GW of additions, accounting for over 60% of global growth." Cumulative wind + solar reached 1.84 TW, accounting for 47.3% of total installed power capacity — surpassing thermal power for the first time ([Gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202501/28/content_WS6798de96c6d0868f4e8ef410.html) [[52](#source-52)]; [Gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202602/12/content_WS698d93cbc6d00ca5f9a091bb.html) [[53](#source-53)]; [PV Magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/01/28/china-adds-315-gw-of-solar-in-2025/) [[54](#source-54)]).
- **The comparison in one number:** China installed ~357 GW of renewables in 2024 alone — roughly 7x the ~49 GW (33.3 GW solar + ~15 GW wind) the entire US added in solar and wind that same year (per ACP/SEIA). A single year of Chinese buildout dwarfs a full year of US additions.
  - **GW vs. TWh — why the "number of homes" differs.** These two units answer different questions, and the home-count only makes sense once you fix the hidden assumption. *GW* is a rate — the maximum power a plant can deliver at any instant. *TWh* is an amount — the total energy delivered over a period. To turn either into "homes" you must assume how many hours the plant actually runs (its *capacity factor*).
    - **The authoritative GW figure:** [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/boom-or-bust-how-to-protect-ratepayers-from-the-ai-bubble/) [[132](#source-132)] states it plainly — a 1 GW data center is "an amount sufficient to power **750,000 homes**." That is the number the industry quotes. But note what it assumes: it treats 1 GW as a *continuous* draw, i.e. the plant running flat-out 24/7. A real renewable plant does not. The same Brookings piece notes that 64 GW of confirmed data-center power projects is "enough to power 56 million homes" — though applying the 750,000-per-GW convention to 64 GW yields 48 million, indicating the 56M figure uses a higher per-GW factor. Either way, the 750,000-per-GW convention is applied to *nameplate capacity*, not to actual renewable output.
    - **The authoritative TWh figure:** the average US home uses ~10,500 kWh/year ([US EIA](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/use-of-energy/electricity-use-in-homes.php) [[131](#source-131)]), so **1 TWh ≈ 95,000 homes** for a year. This is a *delivered* amount, not a peak.
    - **The direct comparison — 1 GW vs. 1 GWh.** This is the cleanest way to see the trap, because the two differ only by the word "hour."
        - **1 GW** is a *rate*: 1,000,000 watts of power, sustained at that instant. It has no time in it.
        - **1 GWh** is an *amount*: 1,000,000 watt-hours — what 1 GW of power delivers if it runs for exactly **one hour**.
        - So **1 GW running for 1 hour = 1 GWh.** Run that same 1 GW for a full year (8,760 hours) and you get **8,760 GWh = 8.76 TWh**. That is the entire bridge between the two units: *GWh = GW × hours*.
        - In homes: **1 GWh ≈ 95 homes** (1,000,000 kWh ÷ 10,500 kWh/home); **8.76 TWh (a full year of 1 GW) ≈ 830,000 homes** — matching the ~750,000 Brookings quotes. But a 1 GW solar plant running at 25% capacity factor only runs "flat-out" for ~2,190 of those 8,760 hours, so it delivers **~2,190 GWh ≈ 200,000 homes**, not 830,000. The gigawatt is the *size of the machine*; the gigawatt-hours are *what it actually produced this year*. Quote the GW and you sound bigger; quote the GWh and you show the real output.
- **China's energy mix for data centers (IEA):**
  - Current: coal ~70% (data centers located mostly in the east), renewables ~20%, nuclear ~10%, gas remainder ([IEA Energy supply for AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai) [[22](#source-22)]).
  - 2030–2035: after 2030 the introduction of SMRs significantly boosts the nuclear share, and by 2035 renewables + nuclear together make up nearly 60% of China's data center electricity supply, pushing coal into decline ([IEA Energy supply for AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai) [[22](#source-22)]). This builds on China's already-operating ~64 GW nuclear fleet (60+ commercial reactors) [[65](#source-65)] and its SMR and thorium programs [[61](#source-61)][[62](#source-62)].
  - **The structural advantage:** China already generates more than twice as much electricity as the US and has increased total power generation by ~6% per year over the past decade, with over half of growth from clean sources (Ember data). The "East Data, West Computing" initiative routes compute to where renewable capacity already exists — a state-coordinated grid-decarbonization strategy with no US equivalent.
- **The carbon emissions divergence:**
  - US: data center growth is met primarily by new and existing gas generation. In the high-emissions scenario, gas dominates through 2035. The US total emissions trajectory for data centers is upward or flat through 2030 ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai) [[22](#source-22)]).
  - China: data center emissions peak around 2030 then decline, because the grid is decarbonizing faster than data center demand is growing. By 2035, 60% of data center electricity is from renewables + nuclear ([IEA Key Questions](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) [[23](#source-23)]).
  - **The irony:** The US, which positions itself as the leader in AI innovation, is building its AI infrastructure on fossil fuels (gas, coal) due to grid constraints and permitting delays. China, which is often characterized as the "world's top polluter," is building its AI infrastructure on a rapidly decarbonizing grid where renewables overtook coal in installed capacity in 2025.
  - [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-will-the-united-states-and-china-power-the-ai-race/) [[40](#source-40)] (Samantha Gross): "the United States largely controls the supply of the most important chips, but China largely controls the supply of renewable energy products, a key means of quickly and sustainably powering them." Kyle Chan adds that this "electron gap" "could potentially reshape the balance of compute for AI between the two countries" [[40](#source-40)].
  - [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-will-the-united-states-and-china-power-the-ai-race/) [[40](#source-40)] (David Victor): "For China, there may be opportunities to use AI-related demands for electricity to make fuller use of power that is already oversupplied in some parts of the Chinese grid."
- **The strategic implication:**
  - The US AI buildout is constrained by the speed of fossil fuel infrastructure (gas turbines with 4+ year backlogs) and slow permitting. China's AI buildout is constrained by the speed of data center construction, not energy supply — because the energy is already being built.
  - China's renewable buildout (350+ GW/year) outpaces the US data center demand growth over a decade by a factor of 2–3. The energy transition is not a constraint for China; it is the enabling condition.
  - The US is effectively choosing between gas (fast but dirty) and renewables (clean but slow to permit). China is choosing between coal (existing) and renewables (already built). The carbon outcomes diverge accordingly.

## Social
In the west, we often hear news reports of people forming intimate relationships with AI leading to romantic entanglements, delusion, and even suicide.  While the frequency of these events has been markedly different in the two countries, China and any country using AI is liable to have these types of incidents occur.  Moreover, China was inspired to take regulatory action from the US suicides rather than any in its own country, while our own country seems to be run by AI companies now.

The primary difference is that the [Chinese government has decided that there are red lines for AI + robotics companies](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/chinese-supreme-peoples-court-legal-guidance-ai-deepfakes) rather than the corporate self regulation approach in the West.  And while I personally struggle to define any of China's AI policies as particularly feminist, the comparative lack of protections against deepfakes leaves women and other marginalized groups especially vulnerable to attack in the west. 

As such, China has:
- Created the legal basis to prevent workers from being replaced by AI
- Banning any product which simulates or replaces human relationships (no AI girlfriend/boyfriends) 
- Provided strong protections against deepfakes initially, and then continued to update the anti deepfake protections as the technology evolved
- Started rolling out universal AI education across the K-12 equivalent system
	- This is not without similar complications as we see in the west via the survey study below
- Started an open international organization to develop AI in the global South

### AI girlfriend/boyfriend ban
While much ink has been spilled about the declining birth rates across the world, China has implemented their regulations against any meaningful replacement of romantic or platonic human relationships. ### Government principles for AI
Superficially, China is known in the west for its "heavy handed" approach vs the largely laissez   faire systems in the west.  Its debatable whether these are true in general, but its certainly clear that China is the only country able to regulate AI anywhere close to the speed of its development.  

It does so primarily by a mix of stated governmental principles and regulations, as well as the legal system.  While very cutting edge compared to the US, even [the EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) [[115](#source-115)] fails to properly keep up with existing developments let alone address future concerns.  I.e. [properly addressing the rapidly developing capabilities of AI models for deepfakes](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/politics/article/3366802/chinas-highest-court-sets-out-new-ai-red-lines-rules-deepfakes-and-privacy).

### AI education and capacity building for the Global South: WAICO vs Pax Silica
Despite the fascist and genocidal overtures of our current moment, soft power is not yet dead.  The two major players right now are Pax Silica and WAICO with a growing amount of incompatibility between the two organizations. 

The US led [Pax Silica](https://stanfordtechreview.com/articles/ai-cold-war-pax-silica-vs-waico) [[97](#source-97)] is a global consortium that seeks to promote US corporate interests around the world with a clear bias towards affluent, global north countries, with an increasingly clear goal of precluding any cooperation with China in the respective countries' AI development [[97](#source-97)][[98](#source-98)].  [The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2026/09/pax-silica-vs-waico-will-central-asia-have-to-pick-a-side/) [[98](#source-98)] frames it as a contest in which Central Asian states may have to pick a side — a framing that undersells how differently the two organizations are actually built.

Conversely, China's [WAICO](https://stanfordtechreview.com/articles/ai-cold-war-pax-silica-vs-waico) [[97](#source-97)] seeks to provide meaningful social, technical, and physical infrastructure for AI development in the global south.  Many have [framed the rivalry as a beginning to a new cold war](https://stanfordtechreview.com/articles/ai-cold-war-pax-silica-vs-waico) [[97](#source-97)], despite the metaphorical shoe not quite fitting.  While neither organization is actually backed by the UN, I do find it notable that [the UN General Secretary visited China for the announcement of WAICO](https://thediplomat.com/2026/09/pax-silica-vs-waico-will-central-asia-have-to-pick-a-side/) [[98](#source-98)].  Also, in contrast to the US, WAICO membership does not preclude any members from joining Pax Silica, which might eventually bring Kazakhstan into conflict with Washington.

Read that table left to right and the point writes itself.  WAICO's entire apparatus is a forum: it can convene, coordinate, and build capacity, but it cannot fine a single developer, withdraw a single model, or compel a single line of code.  Pax Silica is no more binding on its members — its leverage runs through standards, investment, and supply-chain alignment rather than law.  The difference is not in enforcement power but in *audience and orientation*: WAICO is built to serve the Global South's capacity-building needs, while Pax Silica is built to serve US corporate and strategic interests with a clear bias toward the Global North and an increasingly explicit goal of precluding cooperation with China.  That makes the two organizations fundamentally incompatible in practice, even though neither is a regulator in the way the EU AI Act is — a comparison this piece does not need to make, because the real contest on the ground is between Shanghai and Washington, not between Shanghai and Brussels.

### The US market voting with its feet: adoption of Chinese AI models
China is building these open-weight models (AI models whose weights and architecture are publicly released, so anyone can run and modify them) — and the US market is already voting for them. On [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/rankings) [[82](#source-82)], the routing platform that sits between developers and the model providers, Chinese-origin models have carried **at least 30% of US enterprise token volume every single week since February 8, 2026**, peaking at 46% — up from an 11% average over the prior year and just **4.5%** in the first half of 2025 ([CNBC, July 7, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/07/chinese-ai-models-costs-us-openai-anthropic.html) [[83](#source-83)]).  DeepSeek alone is now the **largest single vendor on the platform at 17.6%** of routed tokens, ahead of OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic, with Alibaba's Qwen close behind at 13.9%.

The driver is price.  Open-weight Chinese models run **60% to 90% cheaper** than the leading Anthropic and OpenAI models ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/07/chinese-ai-models-costs-us-openai-anthropic.html) [[83](#source-83)]); DeepSeek V4-Flash lists at $0.14 per million input tokens against $5.00 for GPT-5.5 ([Reuters, August 3, 2026](https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/deepseeks-new-ai-model-is-by-far-cheapest-well-known-models-run-research-firm-2026-08-03/) [[84](#source-84)]).  And this is no longer a developer sandbox story — it has moved into corporate expense reports.  [Lindy, a San Francisco AI startup, moved 100% of its traffic from Claude to DeepSeek in June 2026](https://restofworld.org/2026/when-americans-choose-chinese-ai/) [[85](#source-85)], saving millions, and DeepSeek became the #1 trending software vendor on the Ramp corporate-card index ([Ramp, June 2026](https://www.ramp.com/data/top-saas-vendors-on-ramp-june-2026) [[86](#source-86)]).  Even Airbnb and Anysphere (Cursor) are under congressional investigation after disclosing they built AI infrastructure on Chinese open models like Qwen and Kimi ([Rest of World, June 17, 2026](https://restofworld.org/2026/when-americans-choose-chinese-ai/)).

This is the flip side of the worker-replacement bans above: while China's courts protect its own workers from being displaced by AI, the US market is quietly substituting its own frontier models with cheaper Chinese ones — the US economy is voting with its feet for the very technology the US AI hegemony narrative claims it is outcompeting.


## An AI Wrap-up
- **For the US grid: the data center debate should be a grid debate.** The evidence in this piece is unambiguous: the environmental impact of AI is set by the energy source, not the data center design [[17](#source-17)]. The fastest lever is decarbonizing the grid — which means treating permitting speed, transmission buildout, and renewable additions as national-priority infrastructure questions, not state-by-state permitting accidents.
- **For the US energy buildout: stop betting on gas turbines to power AI.** The near-term US supply plan leans on gas with multi-year turbine backlogs and existing coal and gas plants running hotter [[43](#source-43)][[44](#source-44)]. That is a carbon-increasing path for the technology the US claims to lead. The alternatives are slower to permit but already demonstrated at scale: the renewable buildout China is executing shows what state-coordinated planning can do in a single year [[55](#source-55)].
- **For nuclear: fund the next generation, not the legacy one.** If the US wants nuclear in its AI energy mix, the water and waste math favors the designs with passive safety, recirculating cooling, and reprocessing over once-through LWRs and dry cask storage [[63](#source-63)][[64](#source-64)]. China's ACP100 SMR is already in commissioning and its thorium molten salt pilot is the only operating one in the world [[61](#source-61)][[62](#source-62)]; the US has no operating thorium pilot at all, and its SMR fleet is still in the licensing queue.
- **For labor: the Chinese court rulings are a warning shot, not a curiosity.** Firing workers to replace them with AI is now illegal in multiple Chinese jurisdictions [[78](#source-78)]. The US has no comparable floor, and its own market is already voting with its feet for Chinese open-weight models [[83](#source-83)][[85](#source-85)]. If the US wants to keep the talent it claims to need, it needs a policy answer to displacement before the courts are forced to write it.
- **For governance: pick a model.** WAICO is a forum for Global South capacity building, Pax Silica is a US-led consortium for corporate and strategic interests, and the US is a patchwork that is actively deregulating itself [[97](#source-97)][[98](#source-98)]. None of these is automatically "right," but the US is the only one of the three that has not decided what it wants. That indecision is itself a policy.
- **For readers: the alternative is not hypothetical.** The Chinese model is not perfect — the social tradeoffs in this piece are real — but it is a working, material alternative to the US model, and the US market is already adopting its open-weight output [[83](#source-83)]. If you only follow western media, you are seeing half the story.

# Conclusions?

Other than this piece being too long, it's hopefully given you a material understanding of the Chinese AI model, including how it affects people and the planet.  Their rollout of AI is not perfect by any means, but there's a reason why a majority of Chinese people look forward to the development of AI.  

Similarly, as we increasingly move towards a multi-polar world order, its clear that the US and the west have no intention to help develop the global south after centuries of exploitation and degradation.  Now the Global South is rising to take care of its own needs outside of a decaying post colonial capitalist system. 

When you have a humanistic government guiding the heights and restricting the lows of such a formative technology, it's much easier to envision and subsequently build technology in a way that not only moves China forward, but the world as well.
